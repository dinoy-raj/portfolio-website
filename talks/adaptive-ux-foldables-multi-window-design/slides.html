Five words for every design review, instead of “phone” and “tablet”

⏱ 85s

Retire the words “phone” and “tablet” in design reviews. On Android, an unfolded Pixel Fold in split-screen has the exact same width as a compact phone. Throughout this talk, remember what these five words mean.

▶ CLICK — Window: the viewport allocated to your app, not the physical screen. A tablet might have a large display, but your app could receive only half of it because of split-screen multitasking.

▶ CLICK — Size class: a classification of the available window width and height that tells us which layout applies — Compact, Medium, Expanded, Large and Extra-large.

▶ CLICK — Pane: a self-contained column of related content.

▶ CLICK — Hinge: the physical fold or seam between the two halves of a foldable display. Some hinges are only a visible crease; others physically separate the display.

▶ CLICK — Posture: how the device is being held — flat, folded, tabletop or book.

Rule number one for your next design review: ban the words “phone” and “tablet”.

Window is the space your app actually gets. Size class tells us which layout applies. Pane is a column of content. Hinge is where the screen bends, and where buttons must never die. And posture is how the user is holding it.

Designers have been shipping responsive designs for different widths on the web for years: either stretching to the full width or keeping a minimum width. Adaptive doesn’t mean we stop using responsive techniques.

It means we make meaningful structural decisions instead of simply stretching the existing UI. That brings adaptive layouts, adapting to device postures, making our UI hinge-aware, and deciding how each component behaves on each form factor: multiple panes, navigation that switches.

So building adaptive is a real mental-model shift in how we arrange the UI spatially for each constraint.

Google has introduced a three-step quality roadmap. Think of it as a ladder. Tier 3 is Adaptive Ready: your app fills the window and doesn’t crash or lose state.

This becomes mandatory on Google Play in April 2027. In short: don’t break. Tier 2 is Adaptive Optimised: multi-pane layouts and keyboard and mouse polish, layouts that adapt with the window and use the available space effectively.

Tier 1 is Adaptive Differentiated: custom postures, picture-in-picture and stylus. Hit Tier 2 plus Tier 1, and you earn the Large Screen Badge.

Tier 3 is about reliability. We don’t want an app to become unusable just because someone unfolds their phone or resizes a window. The app should fill the available area: neither overflowing nor letterboxed.

It should preserve user state, including scroll position, text input and media playback. We also need to think beyond individual configuration changes. A user might resize the window, rotate the device and then fold it.

The app has to survive folding, rotating and resizing, even rapid combinations like resize-then-rotate, without losing the user’s work. In multi-window, your app must keep updating even when the other window has focus.

And finally, the basic experience needs to work with external input devices.

Now let’s look at what designers actually own. They need to define layouts for different window sizes. But that doesn’t mean creating five independent Figma screens. They need to define how the experience transforms.

Imagine a messaging app. When the window is compact, we show the conversation list or the selected conversation. When the window becomes expanded, we show the list and the active conversation together.

Now let’s move to the developer side. The first responsibility is to stop assuming the app will always run in one orientation or one fixed window size. Second, we need to preserve state.

In Compose, rememberSaveable is useful for small UI state, while ViewModels help us retain screen state across configuration changes. For more important data, we should rely on repositories and persistent storage.

Third, we must test combinations of configuration changes. Don’t just test folding and unfolding on their own: try resizing, rotating and folding in different sequences. We should also base layout decisions on the current window size rather than hardcoding device-specific logic.

And finally, we need to understand multi-window lifecycle behaviour, and support keyboard, mouse and stylus input.

Here are three layout patterns designers should become familiar with: Material 3’s canonical layouts, proven and standardised patterns designed to adapt. The important thing is that these aren’t tablet-specific layouts.

They are reusable patterns that respond to the space available. The first is list-detail. Think about an email app: the inbox on one side and the selected email on the other.

Hotel bookings and contacts work the same way. The second is a feed: a collection of cards in one or more columns. On a phone we might show one column; as the window expands, we add columns.

Think news, shopping or travel activities. The third is a supporting pane: the primary content stays the focus while secondary information appears alongside it. Imagine a map app: the map is the main experience, with filters or extra information beside it.

Or a document with an outline, an editor with properties.

Navigation is another area where we often see apps simply stretch their mobile interface. On a compact screen, a bottom navigation bar makes sense because it’s easy to reach with the thumb.

But on a tablet, the same bar can consume unnecessary vertical space. A navigation rail is a better fit when more horizontal space is available. On larger windows, an expanded rail can show labels and more navigation context.

One of the easiest mistakes on larger screens is stretching everything. Imagine a login form. On a phone, a full-width input field makes sense. But on a large tablet, a text field spanning the entire display becomes uncomfortable.

The same applies to buttons and reading content. Instead of stretching components indefinitely, designers should define maximum content widths, and developers can implement them with constraints such as widthIn in Jetpack Compose.

The goal is to use the available space without sacrificing usability.

Here’s a subtle design problem. Imagine a foldable display and a primary action button. If we stretch that button across the entire display, the hinge may pass right through the middle of it.

Not all hinges are the same. Some foldables have a visible crease, but the display stays continuous. Others have a separating hinge, where the folding region creates distinct display areas.

One thing I strongly believe is that adaptive behaviour should start in the design system. If every product screen independently decides how navigation, dialogs and sheets behave, we’ll end up with inconsistent experiences.

This way, adaptive behaviour becomes a shared product capability, rather than something every feature team has to reinvent.

Another common mistake is assuming an Android app is always used through touch. Large-screen devices are frequently paired with keyboards, mice and styluses. So designers should specify how the experience works with each input method.

For keyboard users, we need a logical focus order. For mouse users, we need hover feedback and the right contextual interactions. For stylus users, we may need selection, drawing and handwriting support.

Resizable windows mean users decide how much space an app gets. And the range of devices keeps expanding: square foldable displays, wider inner displays, tablets, desktops and emerging XR environments.

If our app assumes a single fixed layout, it’s going to struggle as these experiences become more common.

⏱ 45s

Now let’s look at the timeline. As of November 2026, developers can start publishing foldable screenshots through Play Console, and apps that meet the higher quality tiers can apply for the Large Screen Badge.

In April 2027, Google Play makes Tier 3 the baseline for standard apps: apps must meet the baseline adaptive quality requirements to publish new apps or updates, and there’s a foldable screenshot requirement too.

Then, in August 2027, new and updated apps must target API level 37. That matters, because API 37 removes the remaining opt-out for orientation and resizability restrictions on large displays.

So this isn’t something teams should postpone until the deadline.

I want to close the technical discussion with one thought. Android foldables have been around for several years now. We’ve had time to experiment, learn what works and understand what users expect from these devices.

But even today, we still see apps that treat foldables as regular phones with bigger displays. Imagine a new device category arriving tomorrow. What if, from day one, apps were already designed to take advantage of its capabilities?

What if developers and designers didn’t have to spend years retrofitting existing experiences? That’s the opportunity we have with adaptive design. We already know the lessons. We should start applying them now.
